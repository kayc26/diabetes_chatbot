What is type 1 diabetes?
In type 1 diabetes, the body's immune system mistakenly attacks the insulin-producing beta cells in the 
pancreas. It recognizes these cells as foreign invaders and gradually damages them, resulting in little or no 
insulin production.
When you eat, your body breaks food down into glucose and sends it to your cells. When glucose enters your 
cells, it is either used as fuel for energy right away or stored for later use. Without insulin, glucose builds up in 
the blood instead of fueling the body. That’s why you need insulin every day to replace what your body can no 
longer make. Your goal is to keep your blood glucose within your target range so your body can function well 
and stay healthy.
Keeping your blood glucose in your target range takes a treatment plan and a little detective work to watch 
your patterns. Over time, you’ll start to notice how your blood glucose responds to things like food and physical 
activity from day to day—and you’ll learn how to make adjustments. Your healthcare team is there to help you 
figure out what works best for your needs and goals.
The goal isn’t to have perfect blood glucose levels; it’s to understand and take steps to help you stay in your 
target range. Understanding how food, physical activity, stress, and illness affect your glucose will help you get 
better at spotting your own patterns over time and the steps you can make to stay in your target range.

Treating Hypoglycemia: The 15-15 Rule
When you feel symptoms of hypoglycemia, check your blood glucose level as soon as you can, then treat it right 
away. Ask your healthcare team what they recommend if you ever have the symptoms of low blood glucose but 
cannot check your level right away. Then you know in advance how to stay as safe as possible.
Continuous Glucose Monitoring (CGM) systems read glucose levels from the fluid between your body’s cells, 
called interstitial fluid, and not directly from your blood. CGM readings may lag behind Blood Glucose Monitoring 
(BGM) readings by 5

Ketones and Diabetic Ketoacidosis
If your body doesn’t have enough insulin to use blood glucose for 
energy, it begins using your body fat instead. This process creates 
ketones that can build up in your blood and show up in your urine 
as your body is working to get rid of them. When ketones get too 
high, they can cause a life-threatening condition called diabetic 
ketoacidosis (DKA) that needs immediate medical care.
You can check ketones using urine test strips (20 mg/dL and higher 
warrants attention) or a blood ketone meter (0.6 mmol/L and higher 
warrants attention). You and your healthcare team will develop an 
action plan for when to check for ketones  and what to do if your 
ketone results are high.
You are more likely to develop ketones when:
• Y
our blood glucose levels are above >200 mg/dL
• Y
ou have less insulin in your body, which is 
commonly due to a problem with your insulin 
delivery device or missing an insulin dose
• Y
ou’re sick, vomiting, dehydrated, or under stress
There is is a condition called euglycemic DKA that 
can happen even when your blood glucose is not very 
high, especially if you don't eat enough carbs, take 
SGLT-2 inhibitors, use an insulin pump, or are sick.

Monitoring Blood Glucose
Blood glucose monitoring tools give you the insights you need to make management decisions and spot issues early.
T
here are two main approaches:
Continuous glucose monitors (CGMs) use 
a wearable or implanted sensor to track 
glucose levels in the fluid around your cells 
(interstitial
 
fluid).
These show both the current level and glucose 
trends throughout the day and night and over time.
Glucose readings from a CGM reflect a delayed 5 
minute (or longer) real-time blood glucose reading.
Blood glucose meters (BGMs) measure blood 
glucose with a test strip and require a drop of 
blood for each reading.
BGMs serve as a crucial backup for CGM users, 
especially when symptoms don't align with CGM 
readings. CGMs excel at alerting you to highs and 
lows while showing patterns that fingersticks alone 
might miss. Your healthcare team can guide you to 
the best choice for your needs. 
AID Systems
Many people with type 1 diabetes use an automated insulin 
delivery (AID) system. These systems link an insulin pump with 
a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) and use software to adjust 
insulin delivery automatically based on the CGM readings.
AID systems can help reduce blood glucose ups and downs by 
adjusting insulin delivery around the clock based on your CGM 
data and the settings you and your healthcare team program 
into the device.
These systems automatically handle a lot of your background 
insulin needs, but they still need your help for mealtime insulin and physical activity. For bigger meals, you tell 
the system how many carbs you're eating (or
 
pick "normal," "more," or "less") so it can give the right bolus of 
insulin. For exercise or activity, you turn on exercise mode (or set a higher glucose target) before or during to 
lower insulin delivery and help prevent low blood glucose.
Backup Plans for Technology and Supplies
Even trusted tools can have problems. Keeping extra supplies, such as spare insulin, batteries, and monitoring 
supplies, on hand and having backup methods for checking blood glucose and delivering insulin will give you 
peace of mind. Your healthcare team can help you create backup plans to meet your needs.

Physical Activity and Exercise
People with type 1 diabetes participate in all kinds of physical activity—from everyday movement to competitive 
sports. Being active through NEAT (Non-Exercise Activity Thermogenesis) means enjoying all your everyday 
movements such as walking around the house, standing while doing tasks, fidgeting, gardening or taking the 
stairs which naturally boost your energy, support better glucose control, and help you feel healthier every day. 
Many professional athletes live with type 1 diabetes. Their stories show that it may take some extra planning and 
effort, but you can absolutely reach your fitness level and goals. 
Physical activity helps keep your heart healthy, improves how your body uses insulin, boosts your mood, and 
helps you sleep better. It can also affect blood glucose in different ways depending on how long or how intense 
your workout is. Learning how your body responds to different physical activity is an important part of managing 
diabetes.

Exercise Safety
Before and during physical activity, check your blood 
glucose (blood sugar) and pay attention to trends, 
especially during longer or more intense exercise.
Physical activity can sometimes cause blood glucose 
to drop. If your blood glucose becomes low during 
or after activity, the 15–15 rule can help. This means 
having 15 grams of fast-acting carbohydrate, 
waiting
 
15 minutes, and then checking your blood 
glucose again to see if it has come back up.
After physical activity in the evening or an intense 
workout, keep checking your blood glucose for 
several hours—sometimes even overnight—because 
exercise can cause low blood glucose levels later on. 
Talk with your healthcare team about whether you need 
to make changes to your insulin doses before physical 
activity to help prevent low blood glucose afterward.
Physical activity may also cause blood glucose levels to rise. 
This
 
can happen after high-intensity workouts or during times 
of stress (physical and emotional). Drink water to stay hydrated and 
check your blood glucose again later. Sometimes, levels that rise during 
activity may come down on their own once you stop moving. If your blood 
glucose stays high, follow your treatment plan or contact your healthcare 
team.

and
 
more

glucose levels, so your body may respond 
differently from one workout to the next.

team can fine

tune your plan to keep movement 
both safe and enjoyable.

When you are sick, your blood glucose may rise—even if you are

blood glucose. Checking more often, staying hydrated, and

Insulin doses may sometimes need adjustment, but insulin

patterns. These changes are not always easy to predict and may 
require adjustments over time.

Diabetes Education and Support
Diabetes self-management education and support (DSMES) services provide the knowledge, skills, and ongoing 
support to help you confidently manage type 1 diabetes throughout everyday challenges and life changes.

Growing with Type 1 Diabetes
Type 1 diabetes changes as you do. Your insulin needs, routines, technology, and support may change as your 
body and life change—sometimes gradually, sometimes suddenly. Instead of aiming for one “perfect” plan, 
think
 
of diabetes care as an ongoing process. Review what is working and adjust when needed.
Here are a few ways diabetes care can shift across life stages:
Across all ages and stages, type 1 diabetes affects more than blood glucose. Stress, fatigue, fear of low blood 
glucose, or burnout can show up at any time—and that is normal.
When these concerns arise, talk openly about what you are experiencing. Share concerns with your healthcare 
team, a family member, or a trusted friend. Give yourself grace as you adjust and adapt, one step at a time. 
This
 
is a natural part of living well with type 1 diabetes.
Childhood

often work together. Over time, you

Looking Ahead
Today, more tools and support are available than ever before. Technologies such 
as continuous glucose monitors (CGMs), insulin pumps, and automated 
insulin delivery (AID) systems continue to improve, making
 
blood 
glucose management more precise and less demanding. 
Research is rapidly advancing our understanding, treatments 
continue to evolve, and powerful support networks are 
connecting people with type 1 diabetes around the world. 
Connect your diabetes goals to what you truly value 
and what matters most in your whole life.
When you set goals for managing diabetes, think 
about the things that are most important to you—like 
spending quality time with family and friends, staying 
active and independent, your hobbies, traveling 
without worry, feeling strong and full of energy, or 
being there for the people you love.
By linking your diabetes steps (like checking your 
glucose, choosing balanced meals, moving your body, or 
using your tools and apps) to these big things you value, 
your goals become more personal and meaningful.
When goals connect to what really matters to you, you're more 
motivated to stick with them, and it feels less like "managing 
diabetes" and more like protecting the life you care
 
about.
With the right knowledge, practical tools, and strong support from your team and loved ones, you can make 
small, healthy changes that fit your life. Set goals that matter to you, celebrate every win—no matter how small—
and keep building habits that help you feel stronger and more empowered. Diabetes is just one part of who you 
are—it doesn't define you or stop you from living fully. Take it one positive step at a time.
