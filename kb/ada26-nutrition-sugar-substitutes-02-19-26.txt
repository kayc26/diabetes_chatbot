Understanding Sugar Substitutes
Sugar substitutes are sweeteners that can be used to add sweetness to food instead of table 
sugar. You may also hear them called “artificial sweeteners,” “non caloric sweeteners,” or “zero 
calorie sweeteners.” Most of them have little or no calories and don’t add many carbs.
Regular sweeteners like sugar, honey, syrups, and agave contain fast-acting carbs and can raise 
blood glucose (blood sugar) quickly. Drinks like soda, juice, sweetened tea, and some sports 
drinks can contain regular sweeteners and also high fructose corn syrup, fructose or juice 
concentrate. These drinks can have what amounts to about 10 teaspoons of table sugar per 
serving.
  
If you’re living with diabetes or prediabetes, lowering your sugar and carb intake by choosing 
water, diet soda, seltzer, or unsweetened tea, or sweetening your drinks and other food with 
sugar substitutes, can help lower your sugar intake and keep your blood glucose steady.
Understanding Sugar-Free and No-Added-Sugar Labels
Many foods labeled as “sugar free” or “no added sugar” use sugar substitutes.
▪ Sugar free: The product contains no sugar, but it may contain sugar alcohols or artificial    
   sweeteners.
▪ No added sugar: No sugar was added during processing. However, the food may still contain      
   natural sugars (for example, the sugars found in plain yogurt or milk).
Always check the Nutrition Facts label to see how much “total” and “added sugar” is in a product.
Possible Benefits of Sugar Substitutes
▪ Weight management: These sweeteners have few or no calories. They may help you reduce       
   calories and lose weight, but their long term effect on your weight is still unclear.
▪ Managing blood glucose: Research shows that sugar substitutes won’t cause a large rise in     
   blood glucose levels.
▪ Sweetness without the carbs: Sugar substitutes add sweetness without the added carbs or       
   calories that sugar has. For example, using a sugar substitute in oatmeal can keep the meal       
   sweet while lowering the carbs.

FDA-Approved Sugar Substitutes
These sweeteners are approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA):
▪  Monk fruit: 100–250 times sweeter than sugar
▪  Stevia (Truvia): 200–400 times sweeter than sugar
▪  Sucralose (Splenda): 600 times sweeter than sugar
▪  Aspartame (Equal, NutraSweet): 200 times sweeter than sugar
▪  Saccharin (Sweet’N Low): 200–700 times sweeter than sugar
Since sugar substitutes are so much sweeter than sugar, you don’t need to use as much to get the 
same level of sweetness as regular sugar.
What are sugar alcohols?
Sugar alcohols are carbs that occur naturally in certain fruits and vegetables. They can also be 
produced artificially. Despite the name, sugar alcohols don’t contain alcohol. While they do provide 
calories, they are typically lower in calories than regular sugar. If consumed in high amounts, some 
may experience issues with digestion like bloating, gas, and diarrhea. 
Food labels may use the general term "sugar alcohol" or list the specific name, such as sorbitol. 
The most common sugar alcohols found in foods and other products include: 
▪  Mannitol 
▪  Sorbitol  
▪  Xylitol
▪  Erythritol
▪  Maltitol
▪  Isomalt
▪  Lactitol
Key Takeaway
Sugar substitutes can lower carbs and calories compared to regular sweeteners. Work with your 
health care team if you have concerns about how much you should be using in your eating plan.
