Physical Activity and Exercise
People with type 1 diabetes participate in all kinds of physical activity—from everyday movement to competitive 
sports. Being active through NEAT (Non-Exercise Activity Thermogenesis) means enjoying all your everyday 
movements such as walking around the house, standing while doing tasks, fidgeting, gardening or taking the 
stairs which naturally boost your energy, support better glucose control, and help you feel healthier every day. 
Many professional athletes live with type 1 diabetes. Their stories show that it may take some extra planning and 
effort, but you can absolutely reach your fitness level and goals. 
Physical activity helps keep your heart healthy, improves how your body uses insulin, boosts your mood, and 
helps you sleep better. It can also affect blood glucose in different ways depending on how long or how intense 
your workout is. Learning how your body responds to different physical activity is an important part of managing 
diabetes. 
Here’s how different types of physical activity can help with your blood glucose:
Aerobic activities  
(walking, running, biking, or 
swimming) usually lower your 
blood glucose right away and 
help keep it steadier for a 
good
 
while.
Strength training  
(lifting weights or doing push-
ups) might cause a small rise at 
first, but then it often helps lower 
your blood glucose later as your 
muscles keep working hard.
High-intensity workouts 
(sprinting or fast sports) can 
make your blood glucose go up 
for a short time at the start, but 
they usually bring it down nicely 
for several hours afterward.
The great thing is that exercise can keep your blood 
glucose lower for 24 to 48 hours sometimes even up 
to 72 hours! So, the more you move, the better your 
body gets at using glucose and staying in range.
 Listen to Your Body: Finding 
Movement that Works for You
What matters most is noticing how your body 
reacts to different kinds of movement and finding 
a balance that feels realistic and sustainable for 
you. Mixing up your activities—such as walking, 
stretching, lifting weights, and biking—can help 
support both your physical health and blood 
glucose
 
management.
Everyone’s body responds differently to 
physical
 
activity. Checking your blood glucose 
and paying attention to how you feel can help you 
notice patterns and signs of low blood glucose. 
This awareness can help you make informed 
decisions about food, insulin, and
 
activity.

Getting Started with Movement
If you’re just getting started or want motivation to start without overdoing it, these ideas can help you build a 
routine you can stick with and feel good about.
Everyday Movement Matters
Even short bursts of movement done regularly throughout the day can have big benefits for your health. 
Choosing activities that feel enjoyable, flexible, and realistic will help make 
them a natural part of your routine and support your overall wellbeing.
The movement you do while living your daily life, such as walking around 
the house, doing chores, standing more often, or gardening, all add up 
and count, too.
Physical activity, including movement, can make a big difference in your 
overall health and blood glucose management, especially when done 
regularly. Unlike
 
aerobic workouts or strength training, general activities 
of daily living are usually lower in intensity and happen
 
throughout the 
day. They can help keep your body and joints moving, improve how 
well your insulin is used, and
 
make it easier to stay active without a 
structured routine.
Begin with movement 
or physical activities 
you enjoy, like 
dancing, walking with 
others, or swimming.
Focus on being 
consistent before 
trying to do more.
Note how your meals, 
snacks, and insulin 
timing affect your blood 
glucose during physical 
activity, and if you need 
to, make adjustments 
to help you stay in your 
target range.
You might find it easier 
to set small, specific 
goals, such as “I’ll take a 
30-minute walk with my 
friend after dinner five 
days a week” to meet the 
recommended 150 minutes 
of activity each week.

Exercise Safety
Before and during physical activity, check your blood 
glucose (blood sugar) and pay attention to trends, 
especially during longer or more intense exercise.
Physical activity can sometimes cause blood glucose 
to drop. If your blood glucose becomes low during 
or after activity, the 15–15 rule can help. This means 
having 15 grams of fast-acting carbohydrate, 
waiting
 
15 minutes, and then checking your blood 
glucose again to see if it has come back up.
After physical activity in the evening or an intense 
workout, keep checking your blood glucose for 
several hours—sometimes even overnight—because 
exercise can cause low blood glucose levels later on. 
Talk with your healthcare team about whether you need 
to make changes to your insulin doses before physical 
activity to help prevent low blood glucose afterward.
Physical activity may also cause blood glucose levels to rise. 
This
 
can happen after high-intensity workouts or during times 
of stress (physical and emotional). Drink water to stay hydrated and 
check your blood glucose again later. Sometimes, levels that rise during 
activity may come down on their own once you stop moving. If your blood 
glucose stays high, follow your treatment plan or contact your healthcare 
team. 
Check & Chat
With time, small adjustments, such as 
changing when you take insulin, eat, or move, 
can help physical activity feel safer, easier, 
and
 
more
 
predictable.
Trying different activities and noting how your blood 
glucose responds can help you plan for next time.
Remember, factors such as stress, illness, 
hormones, and lack of sleep can affect blood 
glucose levels, so your body may respond 
differently from one workout to the next.
As you learn your patterns, you and your healthcare 
team can fine
-
tune your plan to keep movement 
both safe and enjoyable.
