Eating with Type 1 Diabetes
What, how much, and when you eat all play a role in keeping your blood glucose steady. There is no one right 
e
ating plan for type 1 diabetes. The key is to create an eating plan that fits your needs while helping you reach 
your health goals. You can work with a registered dietitian nutritionist (RDN) to create an eating plan that works 
best for you.
Carbohydrates (carbs) affect blood glucose the most. How fast and how high your blood glucose rises depends 
on the types of carbohydrate foods you eat and what they are paired with. 
Protein, fat, and fiber are your friends when it comes to eating!
Pairing these helps slow down how fast your body digests 
food, helps
 
you feel full longer, and gives you steady and 
predictable glucose levels instead of quick ups and downs.
Many people find that building meals around vegetables, 
lean
 
proteins, starchy vegetables, beans and legumes, 
fruits,
 
whole grains, and low-fat milk or Greek yogurt 
helps keep blood glucose steadier and supports heart 
health at the same time. Highly processed foods and 
added sugars are not off limits, but
 
noticing how they 
affect your blood glucose numbers and how you feel 
will help you manage your
 
diabetes.
Regular meals, balanced portions, and consistent 
eating patterns support stable blood glucose. 
Matching insulin timing to meals and giving insulin time 
to start working before eating helps avoid sharp rises 
or drops.
Faster-acting carbohydrates, 
such
 
as juice, regular soda, 
or candy, raise blood glucose 
quickly (and can help treat 
lows) but they can cause 
sharp spikes when eaten 
alone (without pairing them 
with protein, fat, or fiber).
Slower-acting choices, 
such
 
as
 
quality carbs like 
starchy vegetables, beans/
legumes, fruits, whole grains, 
and lower-
fat milk or plain 
Greek yogurt (which
 
are high 
in protein), tend
 
to raise blood 
glucose more gradually.
Check & Chat
If you are not sure about the best timing for your 
insulin doses, ask your healthcare team. They 
can guide you based on your insulin type and 
daily
 
routine.
Paying attention to patterns—not seeking 
perfection—makes
 
eating feel more manageable 
and
 
enjoyable.

Drinks and Hidden Sugars
What you drink affects your blood glucose just as much as what you eat. 
Suga
ry drinks such as regular soda, sweetened tea, sports drinks, 
fruit juice, and flavored coffee can quickly raise blood glucose. 
Even drinks that seem healthy may have more sugar than you 
expect.
Caffeine may cause a mild, temporary increase in insulin resistance, 
which can raise blood glucose levels in some people. Not everyone 
responds the same way, so notice how your body reacts.
Alcohol can affect blood glucose, too. If you choose to drink 
alcohol, stay within daily limits (generally no more than one drink 
for women and two for men), check your blood glucose, and talk with 
your
 
healthcare team about how to stay safe if you drink alcohol.
Water is the best choice. Seltzer water or sugar-free drinks can add 
variety when you want a break from plain water. For other drinks, check 
the labels and note how your blood glucose responds so you can 
choose what works best for your body.
