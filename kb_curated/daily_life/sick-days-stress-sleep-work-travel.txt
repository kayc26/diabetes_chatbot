Type 1 diabetes touches every part of daily life. It is there at work, at school, 
during travel, at family events, and on hectic days when everything seems to 
happen at once. Even when you know what to do, real life does not always 
go as planned—and that is okay.
Stress, Emotions, and 
Feeling Overwhelmed
Stress—both physical and emotional—can raise blood glucose levels, even 
when food and insulin have not changed. Worry, tight
 
schedules, conflict, and 
lack of sleep can all affect hormones that influence blood
 
glucose.
Living with diabetes also brings the mental load of planning ahead, checking 
numbers, carrying supplies, and making decisions throughout the day. 
Feeling
 
frustrated or worn out at times is normal. Making
 
small 
adjustments, checking blood glucose more often, getting 
professional support, or simply giving yourself grace can help 
lighten that load.
Illness, Hormones, and Body Changes
When you are sick, your blood glucose may rise—even if you are 
eating less. Fever, infection, pain, and
 
dehydration can all affect 
blood glucose. Checking more often, staying hydrated, and 
continuing to take your insulin are important steps during illness. 
Insulin doses may sometimes need adjustment, but insulin 
should not be stopped.
Hormonal changes, such as during puberty, menstruation, 
pregnancy, aging, and menopause, can also shift blood glucose 
patterns. These changes are not always easy to predict and may 
require adjustments over time.

Work, School, Travel, 
and
 
Daily
 
Routines
Daily life brings practical challenges. Long meetings, shift 
work, delayed meals, travel across time zones, and limited 
access to food can all cause blood glucose to
 
go up 
or
 
down.
Planning ahead helps, but flexibility matters, too. 
Carry
 
your supplies, know your rights to manage 
diabetes at work or school, and have backup plans 
in
 
case of technology or insulin issues.
Many people find that helpful adjustments at 
work, such as having flexible breaks, access to 
food and water, and time to check blood glucose 
or take insulin, can make a big difference in 
feeling confident throughout the
 
day.

Be Ready for School and Travel

22 | American Diabetes Association®
Helpful Travel Extras
Most insulin can be stored safely at room temperature for up to 28 days (some lasting up to 56 days)—check the 
package insert. Avoid
 
exposing it to extreme heat or freezing temperatures. Your specific insulin’s prescribing 
information will have storage guidance.
Planning ahead and packing a little extra can make travel more 
comfortable. Whether you are going across town or around 
the world, travel can shift routines and blood glucose patterns. 
Bringing what you need and checking your health insurance 
coverage before international trips can help you feel confident 
and prepared wherever you go.
When to Ask for Help
If managing diabetes starts to feel overwhelming or your blood 
glucose targets seem hard to maintain, reach out for help. 
Diabetes care is never meant to be handled alone.
Reaching out to mental health professionals to address stress, 
burnout, or
 
other emotions related to managing diabetes is also 
an important part of caring for your
 
health.
For students, working with school staff such as 
nurses, teachers, and
 
counselors can make it easier 
to manage diabetes during the school day. For adults, 
your workplace’s human resources department or 
insurance provider can also help you navigate benefits 
and healthcare coverage questions.
Remember, you do not have to do this alone. 
Building
 
your support team can make living with 
diabetes feel more manageable every day.
