Monitoring Blood Glucose
Blood glucose monitoring tools give you the insights you need to make management decisions and spot issues early.

Continuous glucose monitors (CGMs) use 
a wearable or implanted sensor to track 
glucose levels in the fluid around your cells 
(interstitial
 
fluid).
These show both the current level and glucose 
trends throughout the day and night and over time.
Glucose readings from a CGM reflect a delayed 5

Blood glucose meters (BGMs) measure blood 
glucose with a test strip and require a drop of 
blood for each reading.
BGMs serve as a crucial backup for CGM users, 
especially when symptoms don't align with CGM 
readings. CGMs excel at alerting you to highs and 
lows while showing patterns that fingersticks alone 
might miss. Your healthcare team can guide you to 
the best choice for your needs. 
AID Systems
Many people with type 1 diabetes use an automated insulin 
delivery (AID) system. These systems link an insulin pump with 
a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) and use software to adjust 
insulin delivery automatically based on the CGM readings.
AID systems can help reduce blood glucose ups and downs by 
adjusting insulin delivery around the clock based on your CGM 
data and the settings you and your healthcare team program 
into the device.
These systems automatically handle a lot of your background 
insulin needs, but they still need your help for mealtime insulin and physical activity. For bigger meals, you tell 
the system how many carbs you're eating (or
 
pick "normal," "more," or "less") so it can give the right bolus of 
insulin. For exercise or activity, you turn on exercise mode (or set a higher glucose target) before or during to 
lower insulin delivery and help prevent low blood glucose.
Backup Plans for Technology and Supplies
Even trusted tools can have problems. Keeping extra supplies, such as spare insulin, batteries, and monitoring 
supplies, on hand and having backup methods for checking blood glucose and delivering insulin will give you 
peace of mind. Your healthcare team can help you create backup plans to meet your needs.

What to Expect
Managing type 1 diabetes gets easier the more you practice, just like learning any new skill.
Remember your goal isn’t perfect blood glucose levels. At first, you’ll start noticing how 
your body reacts to insulin, food, exercise, and changes in your daily routine. Over
 
time, 
you’ll keep learning from what happens, try
 
new ideas, and make small changes to help 
keep your glucose levels steady and your routine and choices will flow more naturally. 
Your healthcare team is always there to help you figure things out.
Understanding Your Numbers
Your blood glucose readings provide insights into how your body handles insulin 
and responds to other factors throughout the day and night. They
 
show how food, 
activity, stress, illness, medicine, and timing all work together and affect your blood 
glucose levels. Spotting patterns turns simple data into helpful information for 
safer, steadier care.
Common Times to Check Blood Glucose
Whether you use a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) or a blood glucose meter (BGM), paying attention to your 
numbers can help you spot patterns. A CGM shows how your glucose changes throughout the day and night. 
With a blood glucose meter, checking at the same times each day can help you notice patterns over time.
Look for patterns:
Sleep: 
Before bedtime and 
when you wake up
Eating: 
Before meals and 
two hours after
Symptoms: 
Whenever you sense symptoms 
of low or high blood glucose
Exercise: 
Before and after 
physical activity
Check & Chat
One number tells part of the story, but patterns over time reveal more. If you use a CGM, your healthcare team 
might talk with you about time in range (TIR), which is the amount of time your blood glucose stays in your 
personalized target range.
For most adults, the target range is 70–180 mg/dL. Work with your healthcare team to find your target range 
that meets your needs. Target ranges also differ for children and teens, as well as during pregnancy.
