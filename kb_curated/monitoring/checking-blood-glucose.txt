Monitoring blood glucose

Today, tools such as CGM devices, insulin pumps, and AID systems can be an important
part of diabetes care for many people. You have the right to learn about these tools, ask
if they are right for you, and get help buying and using them.

What can I use to check my blood glucose at home?

You can check your blood glucose at home using a glucose meter or a CGM. Your health
care team can help you choose the right device and show you how to use it.

Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) devices are now commonly used by many people with
type 2 diabetes. They measure glucose in the fluid around your cells and provide a
reading every few minutes. This allows you to see patterns throughout the day and night
and over time, which can help guide your decisions about food, activity, stress, and
medication. Check with your health care team about how much these devices cost and what
your options are.

For people with type 2 diabetes who use insulin, automated insulin delivery (AID)
systems are now a preferred option, especially for people who take multiple daily
injections of insulin. These systems link an insulin pump and a CGM device with software
to adjust insulin automatically based on CGM readings. Talk with your health care team
about whether this approach may be right for you.

What should my glucose targets be?

Your targets will be based on your age, health conditions, risk of low blood glucose,
and treatment plan. The general goals for most adults with diabetes are:

- A1C: below 7% (may vary by individual)
- Before eating: 80-130 mg/dL before meals
- After eating: less than 180 mg/dL 1-2 hours after eating
- CGM users' time in range: spend at least 70% of the day between 70-180 mg/dL

Your health care team may adjust these goals based on your age, health conditions, or
risk of low blood glucose.

Help paying for monitoring and devices

There are several ways to get help with the cost of devices like blood glucose meters,
CGMs, insulin pumps and smart pens:
- Manufacturers often offer savings programs or free trial offers. Ask your doctor or
  pharmacist if the brand you use has a coupon or patient assistance program.
- Copay help is available through nonprofits like the PAN Foundation and the Co-Pay
  Relief Program.
- Community health centers and Medicare/Medicaid counselors may offer extra support
  based on your income or insurance.

Talk to your health care team about the cost of your devices. They can help you apply
for these programs or suggest lower cost options, and help you find a device that your
insurance covers.
