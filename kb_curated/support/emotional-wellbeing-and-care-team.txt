Many people experience what is called diabetes distress—the emotional

Diabetes Education and Support
Diabetes self-management education and support (DSMES) services provide the knowledge, skills, and ongoing 
support to help you confidently manage type 1 diabetes throughout everyday challenges and life changes. 
You
 
will learn practical skills for monitoring blood glucose, using insulin, problem-solving, and coping with 
challenges, gaining tools, and building confidence to live well.
DSMES is not a one-time class. It is an ongoing service that can be especially helpful at key times, such as when 
you are newly diagnosed, starting a new device, or experiencing life changes. You can participate one-on-one 
or in a group. Services are often led by a certified diabetes care and education specialist (CDCES).
To find DSMES services near you, visit our website www.diabetes.org or call 1-800-DIABETES (800-342-2383).

Growing with Type 1 Diabetes
Type 1 diabetes changes as you do. Your insulin needs, routines, technology, and support may change as your 
body and life change—sometimes gradually, sometimes suddenly. Instead of aiming for one “perfect” plan, 
think
 
of diabetes care as an ongoing process. Review what is working and adjust when needed.
Here are a few ways diabetes care can shift across life stages:
Across all ages and stages, type 1 diabetes affects more than blood glucose. Stress, fatigue, fear of low blood 
glucose, or burnout can show up at any time—and that is normal.
When these concerns arise, talk openly about what you are experiencing. Share concerns with your healthcare 
team, a family member, or a trusted friend. Give yourself grace as you adjust and adapt, one step at a time. 
This
 
is a natural part of living well with type 1 diabetes.
Childhood
Parents, family members, caregivers, 
schools, and your healthcare team 
often work together. Over time, you 
may take on more responsibility with 
guidance and support. Growth
 
spurts, 
activity levels, and school schedules 
can affect blood glucose (blood
 sugar),
 
and adjustments are expected.
Teenage years and young adulthood
Hormones, growth, changing sleep 
patterns, stress, and increasing 
independence can affect insulin 
sensitivity and blood glucose levels. 
Balancing diabetes with school, work, 
sports, and social life takes patience, 
flexibility, and backup plans.
Early adulthood  
(college or career start)
Moving out, starting new jobs, changes 
in insurance, and shifting schedules 
may mean managing appointments, 
supplies, and insulin more 
independently. Staying connected 
with your healthcare team, asking 
questions, and building routines can 
increase your confidence and safety.
Pregnancy and family planning
If you are planning a pregnancy, 
talk
 
early with your healthcare team. 
Keeping blood glucose close to target 
helps protect you and your baby. 
Your
 
monitoring needs and insulin 
doses often change before, during, 
and after pregnancy, so regular 
check-ins and support matter.
Mid-life
Hormonal shifts, stress, sleep 
changes, and other health conditions 
can influence blood glucose 
patterns. Regular follow-ups and

Older adulthood
The focus of diabetes care often 
shifts toward safety, comfort, and

dexterity, appetite, memory, or 
financial circumstances may require 
adjustments to tools, routines, 
or
 
caregiver support.

Looking Ahead
Today, more tools and support are available than ever before. Technologies such 
as continuous glucose monitors (CGMs), insulin pumps, and automated 
insulin delivery (AID) systems continue to improve, making
 
blood 
glucose management more precise and less demanding. 
Research is rapidly advancing our understanding, treatments 
continue to evolve, and powerful support networks are 
connecting people with type 1 diabetes around the world. 
Connect your diabetes goals to what you truly value 
and what matters most in your whole life.
When you set goals for managing diabetes, think 
about the things that are most important to you—like 
spending quality time with family and friends, staying 
active and independent, your hobbies, traveling 
without worry, feeling strong and full of energy, or 
being there for the people you love.
By linking your diabetes steps (like checking your 
glucose, choosing balanced meals, moving your body, or 
using your tools and apps) to these big things you value, 
your goals become more personal and meaningful.
When goals connect to what really matters to you, you're more 
motivated to stick with them, and it feels less like "managing 
diabetes" and more like protecting the life you care
 
about.
With the right knowledge, practical tools, and strong support from your team and loved ones, you can make 
small, healthy changes that fit your life. Set goals that matter to you, celebrate every win—no matter how small—
and keep building habits that help you feel stronger and more empowered. Diabetes is just one part of who you 
are—it doesn't define you or stop you from living fully. Take it one positive step at a time.
